# Knowledge Base — Reading and Adapting to Direct vs. Indirect Feedback on Global Teams

**Purpose.** Subject-matter foundation for one micro-learning module. This is reference material for the designer and the build agent — *not* learner-facing copy.

**Objective this KB grounds.** Given a global-team exchange where blunt or vague feedback causes friction, the learner determines whether the cause is a direct/indirect communication difference rather than intent, and chooses a response that fixes the work issue without escalating.

**Scope & boundaries.**
- Covers one dimension: directness of *negative feedback* (Meyer's Evaluating scale), with high/low-context communication (Hall) as the related root.
- Role-agnostic (peer or manager). Audience and scenario specifics are design decisions, not KB content.
- Teaches reading of *observable behavior*. **No nationality mapping** — see §5.
- Deliberately excludes Meyer's other six scales (persuading, leading, deciding, trusting, disagreeing, scheduling) and broader feedback-skills / DEI content.

---

## 1. The core idea

People differ in *how directly they deliver negative feedback*. This is a learned communication norm — not a measure of competence, honesty, respect, or how serious the underlying issue is.

Two ends of the dimension:

- **Direct:** the problem is named plainly, sometimes bluntly; criticism stands on its own with little cushioning; may be voiced in front of others. To someone who expects indirectness, this can read as harsh, rude, or personal.
- **Indirect:** the problem is softened, implied, or wrapped in positives; often delivered privately; sometimes signaled rather than stated. To someone who expects directness, this can read as evasive, unclear, or as "there's no real problem here."

**Where the friction comes from.** Each person interprets the other's delivery through *their own* norm and misattributes the mismatch to personality or bad intent — "they're aggressive" / "they're wishy-washy." The skill is to catch that misattribution: recognize the style gap, then respond to it.

**Two related but separate dimensions** (a common confusion worth getting right):
- *Communicating* — how explicit/literal a message is overall (Hall's low-context vs. high-context).
- *Evaluating* — how direct *negative feedback* specifically is (Meyer's direct vs. indirect).

These don't always line up. A person can be very explicit in normal communication yet relatively soft when criticizing (a frequently cited pattern in U.S. workplaces). For this module, the **Evaluating** dimension — directness of feedback — is the primary lens.

## 2. Observable cues — what the learner reads

The learner can't see anyone's culture; they can only see behavior. These are the concrete tells that signal where a delivery sits on the dimension:

| Cue | Direct end | Indirect end |
|---|---|---|
| **Modifiers around the criticism** | *Upgraders* that strengthen it: "absolutely," "totally," "completely," "strongly" | *Downgraders* that soften it: "kind of," "sort of," "a little," "a bit," "maybe," "slightly" |
| **Cushioning** | Little; the problem is stated outright | Wrapped in positives ("this is great — one small thing…") |
| **Explicitness** | Problem named directly | Problem implied; you're expected to infer it ("interesting choice," "have you considered…") |
| **Setting** | May be given in the group | Usually taken private; public criticism avoided |
| **The requested change** | Spelled out | Signaled, left for you to read |

*Upgraders and downgraders are the single most reliable, observable tell — they sit right in the words.*

## 3. The diagnostic — style difference, or a real problem?

This is the judgment at the center of the objective. The learner is deciding: *is this friction caused by a difference in feedback style, or by genuine bad intent / a real interpersonal problem / feedback that is actually too vague or too hostile to be useful?*

Working heuristics:
- **Pattern vs. departure.** Is this how the person consistently communicates, across topics and people? A consistent style points to a norm. A sharp departure from *their own* baseline may be a real signal worth attending to.
- **Separate content from delivery.** *What* is being said (the work issue) is independent of *how* it's delivered. Blunt delivery doesn't make the point wrong; soft delivery doesn't mean there's no point. Extract the substance first.
- **Check your own attribution.** "Am I reading this as rude/evasive because of *their* intent — or because their style differs from *mine*?" The mismatch usually lives in the gap between two norms, not in one person's character.
- **When unsure, surface it.** A clarifying question resolves ambiguity faster than an assumption (see §4).

**Important boundary:** the goal is *not* to explain away every friction as "just cultural." Sometimes feedback genuinely is hostile, or genuinely is too vague to act on. The skill is to **rule out the style explanation before escalating** — not to excuse everything.

---

## 4. Response moves — what works across the gap

- **Don't reciprocate defensively.** Meeting bluntness with bluntness (or softness with more vagueness) widens the gap.
- **Respond to the work, not the delivery.** Act on the substantive point; don't litigate the tone.
- **Confirm with a closing question.** Especially for indirect feedback, pin down the ask: "So you'd like X changed to Y before Friday — have I got that right?"
- **Flex your own style toward theirs.** Soften and add context with someone who expects indirectness; be more explicit and get to the point with someone who expects directness.
- **Don't try to imitate the other extreme.** A specific trap: if you try to copy a much-more-direct person's bluntness, you tend to overshoot and cause real offense. Adjust *toward* their style; don't mimic it.
- **Depersonalize.** Assume competence and good intent; treat the gap as a translation problem, not a character flaw.

---

## 5. The boundary: tendencies, not rules

This is the credibility line of the whole module.

- These dimensions describe **tendencies at the group level**; **individuals vary widely**, and within-group variation is large. No culture is purely "direct" or "indirect" — it is a continuum, and any one person's style is shaped by personality, role, team norms, and the specific situation as much as by background.
- The country-by-country rankings attached to these frameworks are **contested** — reviewers have questioned how well the classifications hold up empirically, especially for directness. Treat the frameworks as a *lens for noticing*, not a lookup table for predicting.
- **Practical rule: read the person, not the passport.** Don't infer someone's feedback style from where they're from; observe how they actually deliver feedback and adapt to *that*. This is exactly why the module specifies no nationalities — the transferable skill is reading and flexing to observable style, which works regardless of who is in the room.
- A useful reframe for the learner: behavior that looks like disengagement or rudeness (silence, bluntness, vagueness) is often a *different norm* rather than a problem — but it can also genuinely be a problem, which is why the §3 diagnostic comes first.

---

## Implications for the build (KB-level only)

- The scenario must put **observable cues (§2) on screen** so the learner has something concrete to read — the decision can't be guessable from nationality or stated outright.
- The correct path should require **both** moves in the objective: diagnose the cause (§3) *and* select an adaptive response (§4). Rewarding only one is not aligned to the objective.
- Distractors should include the realistic wrong reads: taking blunt feedback personally, dismissing soft feedback as "no real issue," and over-correcting by imitating the other style.
- Keep at least one branch where the friction is *not* purely stylistic, so "it's always cultural" isn't the accidental lesson.
